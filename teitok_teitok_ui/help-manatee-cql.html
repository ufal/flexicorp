Manatee CQL Search Guide

Manatee CQL is the query language used by the Manatee corpus manager and interfaces built on top of it, such as Kontext or NoSketchEngine-style environments. It is closely related to CQL / CQP-style token querying, but it has its own syntax details, operators, and conventions.

Purpose Of Manatee CQL

Manatee CQL is used to describe token-based corpus matches over word forms and annotations. It is designed for corpus search rather than for general programming. A query can match one token, a sequence of tokens, repeated patterns, or structurally constrained contexts.

Typical uses include:

  • finding one word form or lemma
  • finding tokens with a certain annotation such as tag, lemma, or word
  • searching for multi-token patterns
  • restricting results with structural conditions such as within
  • using quantifiers, alternatives, and regular expressions

Basic Token Queries

The most common building block is a token expression in square brackets.

[word="interesting"]
[lemma="go"]
[tag="N.*"]

As in other corpus query languages, the available attribute names depend on the corpus. Common ones include word, lemma, tag, and corpus-specific annotation fields.

Literal Words And Attribute Searches

Depending on the exact environment, many searches are written through attribute constraints rather than quoted bare words. In practice, this means that the most portable form is often:

[word="house"]
[lemma="house"]

Use word when you want the written form as it appears in the corpus, and use lemma when you want all inflectional forms of the same lexical item.

Regular Expressions

Manatee CQL commonly uses regular expressions inside attribute values.

[word="walk.*"]
[lemma="struggl.*"]
[tag="N.*"]

Some common regular-expression symbols are:

  • .: any single character
  • *: zero or more of the previous element
  • +: one or more of the previous element
  • ?: optional, zero or one
  • |: alternative
  • (...): group part of an expression together

Examples:

  • [word="colou?r"] matches both color and colour
  • [tag="V.*"] matches verbal tags when the corpus uses a tagset with that convention
  • [lemma="go|come"] matches either lemma

Combining Conditions On One Token

You can combine multiple constraints inside one token expression.

[lemma="go" & tag="V.*"]
[word="lead" & tag="N.*|V.*"]
[lemma!="be"]
  • & means both conditions must hold
  • | means either condition may hold
  • != means not equal

Sequences

Place token expressions next to each other to search for sequences.

[tag="A.*"] [tag="N.*"]
[lemma="take"] [lemma="place"]

This is one of the most common uses of Manatee CQL: expressing multi-token patterns directly in surface order.

Wildcards, Gaps, And Repetition

The empty token [] means “any one token”. Quantifiers can be used after token expressions.

[lemma="take"] [] [lemma="place"]
[tag="ADV"]? [tag="A.*"] [tag="N.*"]
[tag="A.*"]* [tag="N.*"]
[lemma="go"] []{0,3} [tag="N.*"]
  • ?: optional, zero or one
  • *: zero or more
  • +: one or more
  • {m,n}: between m and n repetitions

Alternatives

You can use alternatives for values or for larger grouped pieces of the query.

[lemma="go|come"]
( [tag="A.*"] | [tag="ADV"] ) [tag="N.*"]

Grouped alternatives are useful when several different local patterns should count as the same broader search.

Structural Restrictions

One of the most characteristic Manatee CQL operators is within, which restricts the match to occur inside a named structural region.

[lemma="go"] within s
[tag="A.*"] [tag="N.*"] within p

Typical region names are things like s for sentence, p for paragraph, or corpus-specific region names defined by the corpus structure.

Other Structural Operators

Depending on the environment and grammar support, you may also encounter operators such as containing or similar structure-related constructions. These are more advanced and not all interfaces expose them equally.

Useful Search Examples

An adjective followed by a noun:

[tag="A.*"] [tag="N.*"]

A lemma regardless of inflection:

[lemma="go"]

A phrase with up to three intervening tokens:

[lemma="take"] []{0,3} [lemma="place"]

A pattern restricted to one sentence:

[lemma="go"] [tag="N.*"] within s

Any number of adjectives before a noun:

[tag="A.*"]* [tag="N.*"]

How Manatee CQL Differs From CQP/CWB CQL

Manatee CQL belongs to the same general family as CQP / CWB CQL, but it is not identical. The overall style is similar, especially for token expressions and sequences, but details of the grammar, operator support, structural commands, and interface-level extensions can differ.

If you already know CQP, you will usually recognize the core ideas quickly, but it is still worth checking the exact behavior of the specific Manatee environment you are using.

Things To Keep In Mind

  • Attribute names are corpus-specific. One corpus may use tag, another pos, another xpos.
  • Regular expressions are powerful, but broad patterns can be expensive on large corpora.
  • within and other structural restrictions depend on the regions actually defined in the corpus.
  • Even when the syntax resembles CQP, the exact advanced operators may differ.
  • When in doubt, begin with a simple one-token query and expand step by step.
